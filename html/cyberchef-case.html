The evidence

Build the briefing

You have five minutes of a C-suite executive's attention. They do not want the hex. They want to know what happened, how bad it is, and what you need them to approve. Structure it like this.

  1. What happened. One or two plain sentences. No jargon.
  2. Impact. Who and what is affected, and how much. Name the data that left.
  3. Root cause. The weak links that let it happen. The evidence points to two: what opened the door, and what made the stolen file readable.
  4. Recommendations. Three, most important first. Each one concrete enough to act on.
  5. The ask. What you need from leadership: a decision, a budget, or a deadline.

Starter recommendations to adapt

  • Stop hardcoding secrets. Move keys into a managed secrets store and rotate them on a schedule.
  • Constrain the assistant. Separate user text from instructions, and never give it a direct path to export the customer table.
  • Shrink the blast radius. Explore ways to compute on customer data while it stays encrypted, so a future breach yields ciphertext instead of records.

That last idea is a live research area: letting a model work on data without ever seeing it in the clear. Your facilitator can say more in a minute.
